# Trak Character — Situational Scenarios (Draft)

Six values, three age bands, two scenarios per band per value (36 total). Structure per the
content rulebook: dilemma addresses the action/situation only, three plausible options (A/B/C)
each with pedagogical feedback (never a grade), one guided reflection question.

For the ~14–18 band, per the rulebook there is deliberately **no single correct answer** — the
feedback lines describe what each choice shows rather than ranking them, and the framing is "the
most values-aligned choice, with reasoning that matters more than the pick."

---

## Composure

### Age ~6–9

**1. The missed shot**

You get a clear chance right in front of the goal and miss. The ball rolls out for a goal kick.

- **A) Kick the ball away in frustration.** This uses up time the team needs and can bring a talking-to from the referee.
- **B) Take a breath and jog back into position.** This gets the team ready for whatever happens next.
- **C) Turn to the teammate who passed it and complain.** This turns one missed shot into two problems instead of one.

*Reflection: What is one thing you can do with your body — like a breath or a stretch — right after a mistake, before the next play starts?*

**2. The referee's call**

The referee blows the whistle for a foul against your team, but you didn't think it was a foul.

- **A) Shout at the referee about the call.** This can get a caution and stops play for even longer.
- **B) Accept the call and get ready for the free kick against you.** This keeps the game moving and keeps focus on what's next.
- **C) Grumble about it to a teammate during play.** This can distract both players from what's happening on the pitch right now.

*Reflection: What could you say to yourself, in your own head, the next time a call doesn't go your way?*

### Age ~10–13

**3. Conceding after a mistake**

Your team concedes a goal that came from a mix-up at the back involving two defenders. The next attack is already coming.

- **A) Point out loudly who was out of position.** This might feel fair in the moment, but it takes attention away from defending the next attack.
- **B) Call out something short and forward-looking, like "reset, next ball," and get into position.** This keeps the team's focus on what happens next rather than what already happened.
- **C) Go quiet and stop communicating for the rest of the half.** This can leave teammates unsure where support is coming from.

*Reflection: After a goal goes in, what is one short, useful thing you could say to the team in the next five seconds?*

**4. The hard tackle**

You go in for a fifty-fifty ball and come away from it in real pain from a strong challenge. The referee waves play on.

- **A) Chase after the player who tackled you to say something.** This can escalate a moment that the referee already decided was fine.
- **B) Signal to the bench that you're hurt and let the referee make the call on stopping play.** This keeps the decision with the person whose job it is to make it.
- **C) Play on through it without telling anyone how much it hurt.** This can turn a small knock into a bigger issue if it isn't looked at.

*Reflection: If you're hurt during a game, who is the first person you should let know, and how?*

### Age ~14–18

**5. Losing badly**

Your team is down several goals with twenty minutes left. Frustration is spreading through the group — sighs, dropped shoulders, players going quiet.

- **A) Keep visibly encouraging teammates even though the scoreline is out of reach.** This can steady others, though it takes real effort to sustain when the result is already decided.
- **B) Focus inward — tighten up your own game, stop chasing the game emotionally, play out the twenty minutes cleanly.** This protects your own performance, though it doesn't address the group's mood.
- **C) Say openly to a teammate or the group that the game has gotten away from you, and suggest refocusing on something specific — like winning the next five minutes.** This names what everyone is already feeling, though it carries the risk that it lands as giving up if the words aren't chosen carefully.

*Reflection: Of these three, which fits how you actually respond under pressure — not how you'd like to respond — and why?*

**6. Captain under fire**

You're wearing the armband. A marginal offside call goes against your team late in a close game, and a teammate is squaring up to the referee about it.

- **A) Physically step between the teammate and the referee and get them to walk away.** This defuses the moment fast, though it can look like you're managing your teammate rather than leading with them.
- **B) Let the teammate vent for a few seconds, then quietly bring them back with something short and direct.** This gives the moment room to pass on its own, though it risks the referee acting before you get the chance.
- **C) Say nothing in the moment and address it with the teammate after the game instead.** This avoids escalating in front of the referee, though it leaves the moment unmanaged while it's happening.

*Reflection: What does the way you'd handle this say about the kind of captain you want to be — not just in this moment, but over a season?*

## Respect

### Age ~6–9

**1. Shaking hands**

The final whistle goes. Your team lost. Both teams line up to shake hands.

- **A) Skip the handshake line and walk straight to the bench.** This misses the moment where both teams show respect for the game.
- **B) Go through the line and shake hands, even though the result stings.** This shows the game matters more than any one result.
- **C) Shake hands but say something unkind under your breath.** This looks like respect from the outside but isn't respect underneath.

*Reflection: What's one thing you could say to an opponent while shaking their hand, win or lose?*

**2. Talking to the coach**

Your coach asks the team to run a drill you don't feel like doing.

- **A) Roll your eyes and do it slowly on purpose.** This makes the drill take longer for the whole team.
- **B) Ask the coach why the drill helps, then join in.** This gets an answer and keeps the session moving.
- **C) Just join in and do the drill properly.** This keeps things simple and lets the team get on with training.

*Reflection: What's a polite way to ask a coach "why" if you're not sure about something?*

### Age ~10–13

**3. Disagreeing with a teammate**

A teammate suggests a tactic during a break in play that you're sure is wrong.

- **A) Tell them flatly they're wrong in front of others.** This can be accurate but makes it harder for them to speak up next time.
- **B) Say what you think and why, directly to them, and let the coach make the final call.** This gets your view heard without shutting theirs down.
- **C) Say nothing and just do it your own way on the pitch.** This can leave the team doing two different things at once.

*Reflection: What's one way to disagree with a teammate's idea without it turning into an argument?*

**4. The louder opponent**

An opposing player is trash-talking your side throughout the match, but nothing they've said has broken any rule.

- **A) Trash-talk back to match their energy.** This can escalate things and pulls focus away from the game itself.
- **B) Ignore it and keep playing your game.** This keeps the focus on football rather than on the other player's words.
- **C) Tell the referee, even though nothing said has broken a rule.** This uses the referee's time on something they can't act on.

*Reflection: What's a way to keep your focus on the game when an opponent is trying to get a reaction from you?*

### Age ~14–18

**5. The unfair opponent**

You're up against a player who's been niggling all game — late challenges, needling comments, nothing quite bad enough for a card. It's starting to get to your teammates.

- **A) Match their intensity so the team sees you're not backing down.** This can rally teammates in the short term, though it risks pulling your own game off track.
- **B) Stay visibly unaffected and let your play do the talking.** This denies them the reaction they're looking for, though it can read as passive if the team needs a response.
- **C) Have a quiet word with your captain or coach so the team's response is coordinated rather than individual.** This keeps things structured, though it takes the decision out of your hands in the moment.

*Reflection: When does standing your ground cross into disrespect, and where do you draw that line for yourself?*

**6. Respecting a decision you think is wrong**

A new coach makes a tactical call — benching a player you think deserves to start — that you privately disagree with.

- **A) Support the decision publicly and save any disagreement for a private conversation with the coach.** This keeps the team unified, though it means holding your view back in the moment.
- **B) Say to teammates that you disagree, so the group knows where you stand.** This is honest, though it can undermine the coach's authority with the team.
- **C) Say nothing to anyone and just focus on your own role.** This avoids conflict entirely, though it doesn't give the coach any feedback at all.

*Reflection: What's the difference between respecting a decision and agreeing with it — and does that difference change what you'd do here?*

## Effort

### Age ~6–9

**1. Tracking back**

Your team loses the ball near the opponent's goal. You're the furthest player forward.

- **A) Stay where you are and wait for the ball to come back to you.** This means one fewer player helping the defence.
- **B) Sprint back to help defend.** This gives the team an extra player at the back when it's needed.
- **C) Jog back slowly, watching the play instead of the space around you.** This gets you back late, after the moment it was needed most.

*Reflection: What's the first thing you do with your feet the second your team loses the ball?*

**2. The tired lap**

It's the last lap of a fitness drill and your legs feel heavy.

- **A) Cut the corner to finish faster.** This means the drill didn't do what it was there to do.
- **B) Slow down but keep going the full way round.** This finishes the job even when it's hard.
- **C) Stop and say you're too tired to finish.** This ends the drill before it's actually done.

*Reflection: What's one thing you can say to yourself to keep going on the last lap?*

### Age ~10–13

**3. Losing badly**

Your team is down four goals with fifteen minutes left and the game feels decided.

- **A) Ease off, since the result looks settled.** This can look reasonable but gives up minutes that are still yours to play.
- **B) Keep pressing and trying the same as you would at 0–0.** This keeps standards the same regardless of the scoreline.
- **C) Stop trying hard plays and just knock the ball around safely.** This avoids mistakes but also avoids competing.

*Reflection: What does "trying your hardest" look like in the last fifteen minutes of a game that's already lost?*

**4. Practising alone**

You've been told your weaker foot needs work, and nobody is checking whether you do the extra reps at home.

- **A) Skip it — no one will know either way.** This means the weak spot stays a weak spot.
- **B) Do a few minutes of it between other things you already do.** This makes the extra work fit into a routine that already exists.
- **C) Do it once, then forget about it for weeks.** This starts the habit but doesn't give it enough time to build.

*Reflection: What's one small thing you could practise for five minutes, three times this week, without anyone reminding you?*

### Age ~14–18

**5. The unglamorous role**

Your coach asks you to play a defensive, unglamorous role for a big game — mostly tracking a dangerous opponent rather than getting forward.

- **A) Commit fully to the role even though it means fewer chances to be noticed.** This serves the team plan, though it may cost you visibility in a game that matters to your own development.
- **B) Do the job but also look for moments to get forward and show what you can do.** This balances both, though it risks doing neither job fully.
- **C) Raise it with the coach beforehand and ask how the role fits your longer-term development.** This is proactive, though it puts the coach in a position of having to justify the plan to you.

*Reflection: How do you weigh what's best for the team this game against what's best for your own development this season — and does that balance ever shift?*

**6. Effort with no audience**

It's a solo training session — no coach watching, no teammates, no one to know if you cut it short.

- **A) Follow the plan exactly as written, even though no one would know if you didn't.** This builds a habit of effort that doesn't depend on being watched, though it takes real self-discipline to sustain.
- **B) Do a shortened version and use the extra time to recover for the next session.** This is a judgment call about load management, though it's also an easy excuse to make too often.
- **C) Skip it and tell yourself you'll make it up later.** This defers the work, though "later" is easy to keep pushing back.

*Reflection: What does the effort you put in when nobody's watching say about the effort you'll put in when it counts?*

## Teamwork

### Age ~6–9

**1. The teammate who lost the ball**

A teammate loses the ball right before the other team scores.

- **A) Point at them and say it was their fault.** This can make a teammate afraid to try again.
- **B) Say "next one" and get on with the game.** This keeps the team's focus on what's coming next.
- **C) Ignore them completely for the rest of the game.** This leaves a teammate feeling on their own.

*Reflection: What's a short, kind thing you could say to a teammate right after they make a mistake?*

**2. Sharing the ball**

You're in space near the goal and so is a teammate who hasn't touched the ball much today.

- **A) Take the shot yourself even though the pass is easier.** This can work out, but it isn't the pass the moment was asking for.
- **B) Pass to the teammate in the better position.** This gives the team the best chance to score.
- **C) Hold onto the ball and wait to see what happens.** This can let the moment pass for everyone.

*Reflection: How do you decide, in a split second, whether to pass or shoot?*

### Age ~10–13

**3. The new player**

A new player joins the squad partway through the season and doesn't know anyone yet.

- **A) Let them find their own way in — it's not really your job.** This leaves someone to work it out entirely alone.
- **B) Introduce them to a couple of teammates and include them in warm-up.** This gives them an easier way into the group.
- **C) Only talk to them if the coach asks you to.** This makes any inclusion feel required rather than genuine.

*Reflection: What's one specific thing you could do this week to help a new teammate feel part of the group?*

**4. Splitting credit**

Your team wins after a move you set up, but a teammate gets praised for the finish and no one mentions the pass.

- **A) Point out afterward that you set it up.** This is honest about your part, though it can come across as needing credit.
- **B) Let it go and celebrate the goal as a team result.** This keeps the focus on the team outcome rather than individual credit.
- **C) Bring it up with a coach privately, not to complain, but to check they noticed.** This gets it acknowledged without making it a public issue.

*Reflection: Does it change how you play if no one notices your part in a goal? Why or why not?*

### Age ~14–18

**5. The unequal teammate**

A teammate is clearly struggling this season — losing confidence, making more mistakes, being subbed early. The team's results are on the line in a run of tight games.

- **A) Cover for their mistakes on the pitch without saying anything to them directly.** This protects them in the short term, though it doesn't address what's causing the dip.
- **B) Talk to them directly and ask how they're doing, even though it's an awkward conversation.** This addresses it honestly, though it risks the conversation landing badly if the timing or words are off.
- **C) Say nothing and let the coach handle it — it's not your role.** This respects the coach's authority, though teammates often notice things a coach doesn't see from the touchline.

*Reflection: Where's the line between a coach's job and a teammate's job when someone on the team is struggling?*

**6. Team goal vs. personal goal**

You have a genuine chance to score a personal milestone (a hat-trick, a goal tally, a scholarship-relevant stat) but the higher-percentage play in the moment is to pass to a better-placed teammate.

- **A) Take the shot — the milestone matters to your future and the position is still legitimate.** This is defensible, though it puts a personal stake ahead of the team's best option in that instant.
- **B) Make the pass, because it's the better team option regardless of what it costs you personally.** This puts the team play first, though it means giving up something that mattered to you.
- **C) Make the pass this time, but be honest with yourself and your coach afterward about how much the milestone matters to you.** This tries to hold both things at once, though it takes real self-awareness to do without resentment building.

*Reflection: How do you want to be able to answer, years from now, when someone asks how you handled moments like this?*

## Responsibility

### Age ~6–9

**1. Forgotten kit**

You forget to bring your shin pads to training.

- **A) Say a teammate must have taken them.** This blames someone else for something that wasn't their doing.
- **B) Tell the coach you forgot them and ask what to do.** This owns the mistake and gets it sorted quickly.
- **C) Say nothing and hope no one notices.** This leaves the problem unresolved and hoping it goes unseen.

*Reflection: What's one thing you could do the night before training so you don't forget your kit again?*

**2. The knocked-over cones**

You're running a drill and accidentally knock over a stack of cones, and no one saw it happen.

- **A) Leave them and carry on with the drill.** This leaves a mess for someone else to deal with.
- **B) Stop and pick them back up before continuing.** This fixes the mistake even though nobody would have known.
- **C) Kick a few more over so it doesn't look like just you.** This turns one small accident into a bigger one.

*Reflection: What's one thing you could fix yourself, without being asked, next time something like this happens?*

### Age ~10–13

**3. The missed session**

You skip an optional extra training session without telling your coach, and a teammate covers for you when asked where you were.

- **A) Let the teammate's cover story stand.** This solves the immediate awkwardness but leaves someone else holding your excuse.
- **B) Tell the coach yourself what actually happened.** This puts the responsibility back where it belongs.
- **C) Avoid the coach for a few days until it blows over.** This delays the issue rather than dealing with it.

*Reflection: What's the difference between a mistake you own up to and one you let someone else cover for?*

**4. Group project, one no-show**

Your five-a-side team was meant to bring a shared piece of equipment between you, and it was your turn — you forgot.

- **A) Say the group should have reminded you.** This puts the responsibility on everyone but yourself.
- **B) Apologise to the group and sort out a fix for next time, like a shared reminder.** This owns the miss and prevents it happening again.
- **C) Avoid mentioning it and hope someone else brings something similar.** This leaves the actual gap unaddressed.

*Reflection: What's one system — like a note or a reminder — that would stop this happening again?*

### Age ~14–18

**5. Owning a costly mistake**

You give the ball away in your own half in the final minutes of a game your team goes on to lose.

- **A) Say clearly to the team afterward that the mistake was yours.** This is direct and accountable, though it can be a hard thing to say out loud in a raw moment.
- **B) Let the result speak for itself and address it in private with your coach instead.** This avoids performing contrition in front of the group, though the team may be left without closure.
- **C) Focus the post-match conversation on what the team can learn from the sequence as a whole, without singling out your own role or anyone else's.** This keeps the focus constructive, though it can look like sidestepping ownership if not handled carefully.

*Reflection: What does taking responsibility for a mistake actually look like once the whistle's gone and the moment's passed?*

**6. Responsibility beyond yourself**

As one of the older players, you notice a younger teammate has started cutting corners in training — turning up late, not finishing drills — and no adult has flagged it yet.

- **A) Say something directly to the younger player yourself.** This addresses it early, though it isn't formally your role and could land wrong coming from a peer.
- **B) Mention it to the coach so they can decide how to handle it.** This routes it through the right channel, though it takes the decision out of your hands.
- **C) Leave it — it's not your job to police other players' effort.** This avoids overstepping, though it lets a pattern continue unaddressed.

*Reflection: Where does your responsibility to the team end and someone else's responsibility to lead begin?*

## Sportsmanship

### Age ~6–9

**1. Winning big**

Your team wins by a lot of goals against a team that's clearly struggling.

- **A) Keep celebrating every goal the same way as the first.** This can feel like rubbing in the score to the other team.
- **B) Tone down the celebrations once the score is already well ahead.** This shows the win still matters without making it harder for the other team.
- **C) Say something to the other team about how easy the game was.** This can make an opponent feel worse than the scoreline already does.

*Reflection: What's a way to celebrate a goal that doesn't make the other team feel bad about the score?*

**2. Losing**

Your team loses the game and some players are upset walking off.

- **A) Blame the referee to your teammates.** This looks for a reason outside the team rather than accepting the result.
- **B) Say "good game" to the other team and talk about it with your coach after.** This accepts the result and keeps the conversation for the right time and place.
- **C) Throw your boots down at the side of the pitch.** This shows the frustration but doesn't help anyone deal with the result.

*Reflection: What's one thing you can say to yourself after a loss to help you feel ready for the next game?*

### Age ~10–13

**3. The opponent's injury**

An opposing player goes down injured during a promising attack your team was building.

- **A) Play on since the referee hasn't stopped it.** This is within the rules but can leave an injured player waiting.
- **B) Kick the ball out so the game can stop for the injury.** This puts a person's wellbeing ahead of the passage of play.
- **C) Slow down without fully stopping, to see how badly hurt they are first.** This can create confusion about whether play has actually stopped.

*Reflection: What would you want an opponent to do if it were you on the ground?*

**4. Winning on a bad call**

Your team scores from what you're fairly sure was actually offside, and the referee doesn't catch it.

- **A) Celebrate normally and say nothing.** This takes the goal as it's given, without acting on what you noticed.
- **B) Mention to the referee or your captain that you thought it was offside.** This puts honesty about the moment ahead of the result.
- **C) Celebrate extra hard specifically because you got away with it.** This turns a lucky break into something to make a point of.

*Reflection: Does it matter whether a goal was fair if the referee allows it anyway? Why or why not?*

### Age ~14–18

**5. Winning humbly**

Your team wins a rivalry match convincingly after a build-up full of trash talk from both sides in the lead-up.

- **A) Let the result do the talking and keep the post-match interaction with the other team minimal but respectful.** This is restrained, though it can look distant if the other team wants more engagement.
- **B) Go out of your way to shake hands and acknowledge the opposition individually, given how much was said beforehand.** This actively closes the gap the trash talk opened, though it takes a level of composure that's hard to manage right after a big win.
- **C) Enjoy the win openly with your own team and let the opposition have their own space to process the loss.** This respects both sides' need for their own moment, though it can be read as indifference if not paired with any acknowledgment.

*Reflection: After a result like this, what do you actually want the opposition to remember about how your team won?*

**6. Losing with dignity**

Your team loses a match that eliminates you from a competition you'd trained toward all season, in the last minute, on a decision that could have gone either way.

- **A) Congratulate the opposition immediately and fully, even though the disappointment is real.** This models the standard in the moment, though it can mean not processing your own feelings until later.
- **B) Take a few minutes alone first, then go and congratulate the opposition once you can do it properly.** This is honest about needing a moment, though it risks the gesture feeling delayed or obligatory.
- **C) Keep your reaction low-key and professional with the opposition, and save any real reaction for your own team afterward.** This separates public and private processing, though it can come across as guarded to the players you just competed against.

*Reflection: Is there a "right amount of time" to feel disappointed before showing sportsmanship, or does that depend on the person?*

## Notes for psychologist review

Reviewed against the four overriding rules in `character-content-rules.md` before this draft was
committed. Rules 1 (never identify the child with the mistake), 2 (never grade/score/rank), and 4
(guided, specific reflection questions) read as consistently followed across all 36 scenarios on a
full re-read.

**Rule "no clean answer" for the ~14–18 band needs your specific attention on two scenarios**,
flagged during drafting and confirmed on review rather than silently smoothed over:

- **Respect #6** ("Respecting a decision you think is wrong") — option A (support publicly,
  disagree privately) reads as the implicitly favoured answer; B and C carry comparatively harsher
  downsides ("undermine the coach's authority," "doesn't give the coach any feedback at all").
  Worth rebalancing the downsides so none of the three reads as the "textbook" answer.
- **Composure #6** ("Captain under fire") — option C's downside ("leaves the moment unmanaged
  while it's happening") reads as more clearly negative than A's or B's, which may unintentionally
  steer away from C.

Every other ~14–18 scenario was checked for the same pattern and reads as genuinely open on this
review, but a second, expert read is still the right call before any of this ships — that's the
whole point of this draft existing.
